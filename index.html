These are preparation lenses, not a published Oliver Wyman scoring rubric. The firm says conversational interviews explore your accomplishments, interests and career aims; case interviews explore how you approach and evaluate problems.

  • Answer the question directly; then show the situation, your action, result, and lesson.
  • Distinguish what you did from work completed by the team.

  • Don't invent a number, decision, outcome or personal role to strengthen a story.

Frameworks

STAR
Most “tell me about a time” questions. Keep Situation and Task short. Spend most time on Action (you) and Result (numbers).
SOAR
Failures and pressure. Situation, Obstacle, Action, Result. Lead with the real difficulty.
STARR
“What did you learn?” STAR plus one line on what you would do differently.
PREP
Motivation and opinions. Point, Reason, Example, Point. Answer first.

What is your biggest weakness? (about 55 seconds)

One thing I've been working on is making sure I involve stakeholders early enough, rather than getting too focused on the analytical side of a problem.

For example, during my internship at P&G, I initially focused on developing what I thought was the best solution for the distributor inventory planning process. But when I presented it to the category teams, they raised concerns about how it would fit into their existing workflows.

That made me realize that even if a solution makes sense analytically, it won't necessarily work in practice unless you understand the needs of the people using it.

So I took their feedback on board, incorporated more flexibility into the model, including manual overrides, and refined the solution.

Since then, I've made a conscious effort to engage stakeholders earlier. For example, when planning the AI-led workshops at SAP, I made sure to align with all the relevant members of the workshop team from the beginning, rather than developing the approach in isolation.

Key message: I recognized a tendency to focus on analysis before stakeholder alignment at P&G, adapted my solution to operational needs, and applied the lesson by engaging the SAP workshop team from the beginning. Keep the example factual and concise.

1. P&G — impact and stakeholder influence AchievementInfluence

Talk track: During my P&G internship, I was asked to develop an approach to improve distributor inventory planning. Some products were running out of stock while others were overstocked and at risk of expiry. I redesigned the process and wanted to take it beyond a theoretical framework. But when I showed the model to category teams, I received significant pushback: they needed flexibility for promotions and category-specific realities that my initial version didn't fully capture. I worked through their concerns, incorporated controlled manual overrides, and tailored the solution to operational requirements. I also automated reporting using Power BI and Power Automate. The process reduced reporting time by 80%, and our modeling projected a 27% reduction in expiry-related costs. Leadership approved the solution for immediate implementation and incorporated it into the following fiscal year's operating strategy.

What to defend: 27% is forecast, not achieved savings; 80% is the achieved reporting-time improvement. You developed the initial solution before involving category stakeholders sufficiently, then corrected that. “Approved for immediate implementation” does not mean every part was already deployed.

Follow-ups: Who objected and why? How did manual overrides work? What changed in your model? What was the baseline for 27% and 80%? Who confirmed the “first intern in the region” claim in your introduction?

2. Rahatak — resolving competing priorities TeamworkConflict

Talk track: During the INJAZ entrepreneurship competition, I worked on Rahatak with a team of eight people from computer engineering, business and industrial engineering. We struggled with different priorities. The computer engineers focused on the product and launch features, the industrial engineers emphasized customer needs and cost, and the business members focused on reporting and the storyline. I organized meetings to give everyone space to explain their perspective. I then introduced a shared North Star: we agreed on the ultimate goal, translated it into objectives and metrics, and assigned owners. That gave us clearer priorities and helped us work together rather than in parallel. We ultimately placed second out of 36 teams at the UAE stage. I learned that disagreement isn't always resolved by choosing one side; sometimes it's resolved by creating a common basis for decisions.

Watch out: This is a story about conflicting functional priorities, not necessarily a serious personal dispute. Don't claim there was a dramatic interpersonal confrontation.

Follow-ups: What was the North Star in concrete terms? Which objective won priority? How were owners assigned? What changed in daily work after alignment?

3. Rahatak — leadership variation LeadershipBackup

Talk track: As CFO of Rahatak, I worked in an eight-person team with very different backgrounds. Everyone was contributing, but it became clear that we weren't optimizing for the same priorities. I took the initiative to bring the team together, hear each perspective, and establish a shared goal. From there, we broke the work into objectives, measurable outcomes and individual owners so that people understood how their contributions fit together. Priorities became clearer, coordination improved, and we finished second out of 36 teams at the UAE stage. It taught me that leadership can mean enabling good decisions and helping others work effectively—not simply telling people what to do.

Use deliberately: This overlaps with Story 2. Prefer the SAP workshop story as your primary initiative/leadership example; use this variation only when a question specifically asks about leading a multidisciplinary team.

Follow-ups: How did you persuade the team to use your framework? What resistance did you receive? What were you accountable for as CFO?

4. Rahatak — strategic pivot and commercial judgment AdaptabilityAnalysis

Talk track: During Rahatak, I served as CFO for a startup concept offering an AI-assisted wheelchair service in airports. Our initial plan was to charge passengers, but we learned that dnata already offered a similar service for free. That forced us to reconsider the commercial model. I developed an advertising-led alternative designed to keep the service free for both passengers and airports. To assess its potential, we benchmarked airport ad prices and modeled 80% occupancy of available advertising space—below the full utilization we observed in existing airport advertising. For demand, we used flight volumes and publicly available estimates of the share of travelers needing mobility assistance, then applied a 75% potential-adoption assumption based on customer acceptance surveys. That gave us a more evidence-based proposal. We presented the revised business model and finished second out of 36 teams at the UAE stage.

Critical definitions: 80% is projected advertising-space occupancy, not wheelchair utilization. 75% is projected adoption among wheelchair-service users, not fleet size or measured adoption. Current airport ad occupancy was observed as fully used; this did not guarantee sales for wheelchair advertising.

Follow-ups: Who would be the advertisers? Why would passengers/brands notice ads on wheelchairs? How did you translate passenger counts into addressable users? What share of those could you serve? What costs and risks were in the model? How sensitive is the outcome to 50% ad occupancy or adoption?

5. Rahatak — a genuine mistake / failure answer FailureAccountability

Talk track (about 55–70 sec): One mistake I learned from was during Rahatak, when I was CFO of our airport wheelchair-service concept. We initially built our approach around charging passengers, without pressure-testing the free alternatives thoroughly enough. During an early pitch, we learned that dnata already offered a comparable service for free. That was a significant flaw in the commercial premise, and as CFO I felt responsible for helping us address it rather than defending the original idea. I revisited the revenue model and proposed that we keep the service free for users and the airport, with revenue coming from advertising. We benchmarked ad pricing and used demand and adoption assumptions to model the alternative. We were able to incorporate the new approach into our proposal and eventually placed second out of 36 teams. The lesson for me was to check the biggest assumption—especially the competitive alternative—before committing too much analysis to a solution.

Why this is a defensible failure story: The initial pricing assumption failed a commercial reality check; the entire project did not fail. The personal ownership is taking responsibility for the financial model as CFO and changing course. Do not say you alone chose the initial paid model, lost customers, or launched an unsuccessful product.

Follow-ups: What would you have researched first? Why wasn't the free alternative identified sooner? What assumptions remain risky in the advertising model? What did you learn about receiving tough feedback?

6. Noon Minutes — unexpected materials shortage SetbackCollaboration

Talk track: During my Industrial Engineering capstone, our team worked with Noon Minutes to design a reusable delivery tote. The materials we'd ordered for the prototype did not arrive due to regional conflict, and we couldn't source the same materials locally. As a team, we adapted the plan and built a functional prototype with locally available substitutes. That let us demonstrate the form and intended functionality, but we were careful not to claim that it validated all performance requirements. In particular, temperature control and cleanability still needed testing with the intended materials. We documented the theoretical analysis and proposed next steps for Noon Minutes to assemble and test the correct-material prototype. We were able to demonstrate our design and clearly explain the limits of what we'd validated. That experience reinforced the importance of being resourceful while staying honest about evidence.

Your role: This was genuinely collective work. Say “we worked on the workaround together”; don't claim you independently sourced materials or led the decision. If asked about your contribution, explain that you participated in the shared design, prototype and documentation work, without taking sole credit.

Follow-ups: Which required test could not be performed? What could the substitute prototype demonstrate? What exactly did the theoretical work cover? How would the final design be validated?

7. SAP AI workshops — initiative and sustainable impact LeadershipInnovation

Talk track: At SAP, I worked within the Chief Revenue Office on AI adoption as well as go-to-market and business planning. One opportunity was to help the team run AI-led workshops in a more repeatable way. Before developing the approach, I aligned with the relevant workshop members and consulted colleagues with technical expertise, including IT, so that the process would fit the team's needs and approved tools. I helped pilot the workshop approach and created a standard operating procedure describing how to run it. The approach was successful, and two subsequent workshops have followed that SOP. What I particularly value about that result is that it wasn't just a one-off session; it became a method others could reuse. It also reinforced the lesson I'd learned at P&G about involving stakeholders before building the solution.

Accuracy: Two subsequent workshops used the approach; don't infer company-wide adoption, identical results or quantified time savings without evidence. Keep confidential internal tools and data out of the interview.

Follow-ups: What was the workshop intended to achieve? Which step changed from the original process? How did you select the tools? What human review did the SOP require? What feedback showed the first workshop was a success?

8. SAP 2027 budget — ambiguity and senior stakeholder JudgmentStakeholders

Talk track: At SAP, I worked directly with a global sub-solution Chief Revenue Officer on a proposal for his 2027 solutions sales budget. The task was open-ended: recommend a number and make a defensible case for it, without being given a fixed method. I structured the work around historical performance, market research and input from strategy and operations. I used that evidence to develop a budget rationale and a clear storyline, then iterated the proposal through several rounds of feedback from the CRO. The biggest challenge was turning incomplete information into a recommendation senior leaders could interrogate. The proposal progressed through the review process, although final approval was still pending when I last discussed it. I learned to make assumptions explicit and communicate the logic behind a recommendation, not just the number.

Follow-ups: What were the competing methods? Which assumption mattered most? What feedback changed the model? How did you handle uncertainty? How did you decide what could be shown to leadership?

9. 180 Degrees — pricing strategy ConsultingCommercial

Talk track: At 180 Degrees Consulting, I worked on pricing strategy for an online QR-menu platform. The challenge was recommending a practical pricing structure when competitors used different models and potential restaurant clients could hesitate to pay before seeing value. We considered how to reduce the barrier to adoption while still creating a path to revenue. The recommendation was a lower initial fee paired with a transaction-linked percentage that stepped down as customer sales grew. This helped balance adoption incentives with the platform's opportunity to participate in customer growth. I learned how pricing design is about customer behavior and incentives, not just calculating a single number.

Accuracy: This was a recommendation. Don't claim the client implemented it or that it increased sales without confirmation.

Follow-ups: How did you benchmark competitors? Why a declining percentage? What would you test with restaurant owners? How would you set pricing tiers and break-even points?

Behavioral practice

Choose a random question; answer without notes in roughly 60–90 seconds. Open the card to compare your answer against what matters, then tackle the follow-ups aloud. In four interviews, choose different examples when they fit better.

1. Tell me about yourself.
Use: Approved introduction (Intro tab), about 2 minutes. Probes: Why Industrial Engineering? What did you personally own at P&G? Why consulting now?
2. Why consulting? Why Oliver Wyman? Why Dubai?
Use: Three separate concise answers in Intro tab. Avoid reciting the full P&G story again. Probes: Why not SAP? Why not another consulting firm? What attracts you outside AI? What do you know about Quotient?
3. What are your biggest strengths and weakness?
Use: Intro tab. Strengths: adaptability, analytical problem solving, collaboration; support with a concrete story. Weakness: you engaged P&G stakeholders too late, corrected the model, and later involved the SAP AI workshop team earlier. Probe: What do you still actively need to improve?
4. Tell me about a time you resolved disagreement.
Use: Rahatak story 2 (eight people with different priorities). Probes: What was the North Star? What actually changed? Were there any interpersonal tensions? Don't invent a confrontation.
5. Tell me about a time you demonstrated leadership.
Use: SAP AI workshops story 7; Rahatak story 3 as backup. Probes: What did you initiate? How did you gain buy-in? What continued after you left the room?
6. Tell me about a time you influenced people who disagreed.
Use: P&G story 1. Probes: Why were the category teams skeptical? What specific trade-off did you make? How did you preserve analytical discipline with manual overrides?
7. Tell me about a time you made a mistake or failed.
Use: Rahatak story 5. State the mistake directly: not pressure-testing the competitive free alternative early enough. Don't imply the whole business launched and failed. Probes: What early check would have prevented it? Which assumptions remained untested after pivoting?
8. Tell me about a time you changed direction based on new evidence.
Use: Rahatak story 4. Probes: What does 80% utilization mean? What does 75% adoption mean? How did you use flight volumes and mobility statistics? What would you do if ad occupancy dropped to 50%?
9. Describe an unexpected setback and how you handled it.
Use: Noon Minutes story 6. Probes: What could you demonstrate with alternate materials? What couldn't you validate? How did the team decide the next steps? What was your individual contribution?
10. Give an example of creating lasting change.
Use: SAP AI workshop story 7. Probes: Why was an SOP needed? Who else used it? Two subsequent workshops followed it—what did you learn from that?
11. Tell me about working with ambiguity or senior stakeholders.
Use: SAP budget story 8. Probes: What assumptions drove the number? What feedback did the CRO give? How did you choose among alternative methods? Avoid confidential numbers.
12. Walk me through a market size or commercial recommendation.
Use: 180 Degrees stories 9 and 10. Probes: Show the market-sizing equation, explain scope and double-counting, or defend transaction-linked pricing against a flat fee.
13. How do you handle competing deadlines and pressure?
Partially prepared: Older notes mention simultaneous ArcelorMittal work, Rahatak CFO duties and coursework. You have not yet provided a concrete decision about priorities or a deadline. Don't give a polished invented story. In practice, choose one real week and explain the deadline, priority logic, trade-off, communication and outcome. Alternative: Noon Minutes gives a genuine setback but not necessarily competing deadlines.
14. Where do you see yourself in five years?
Use: Intro tab. Focus on client problem ownership, judgment, supporting teams and long-term learning rather than a specific title.
15. How do you use AI, and where can it fall short?
Use: SAP AI story 7. Explain the SOP and where human checking is necessary. Discuss privacy, errors and fit for purpose as considerations, without pretending those checks occurred if they did not.
16. What do you know about Quotient?
30-second answer: It's Oliver Wyman's offering combining strategy, technology and AI implementation for business outcomes. Mention a verified client example from the Intro tab and connect it to the practical adoption challenge you experienced at SAP. Avoid: Calling the global leader Dubai-based or implying you're joining an AI-only role.
Interviewer follow-up rule: For every answer, be able to say: (1) what I personally did, (2) what other options we considered, (3) what the number actually measures, (4) what remains unproven, and (5) what I would do differently.

  • 180 Degrees: Pricing and sizing were separate client projects. Don't claim either client adopted the advice without knowing.

Questions worth asking interviewers

  • What does a strong first six months look like for an entry-level consultant on your team?
  • What helps new consultants contribute meaningfully to case teams early?
  • How do consultants here balance analytical rigor with practical client implementation?
  • What has surprised you most about the kinds of challenges clients in this region are bringing to the firm?
  • If relevant to the interviewer: how is practical AI adoption changing the way your team works?

Choose two thoughtful questions based on the interviewer and time. Don't read a scripted list, and don't ask questions already answered during the discussion.

Delivery checklist

Four-interview rehearsal plan

  1. Record the approved introduction once, aiming for a relaxed two-minute explanation.
  2. Say Why Consulting, Why Oliver Wyman, weakness and strengths without a script; aim for 30–60 seconds each.
  3. Rehearse P&G, Rahatak conflict, Rahatak pivot/failure, Noon Minutes, SAP AI and SAP budget. Start with a 60-second answer and allow follow-ups.
  4. Across four simulated rounds, rotate examples when appropriate; avoid answering every question with P&G or Rahatak.
  5. For each story, practice one skeptical question about your assumptions and one about your personal contribution.
  6. Don't use an unverified deadline story. Prepare an actual competing-priorities incident before the interview if you can recall one.
